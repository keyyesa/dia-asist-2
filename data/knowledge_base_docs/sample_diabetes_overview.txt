Diagnostic Criteria
Diabetes is generally diagnosed using one of the following measures: a fasting
blood glucose (FBG) of 126 mg/dL or higher, a 2-hour plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL
or higher during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), a random blood glucose
(RBG) of 200 mg/dL or higher together with classic symptoms, or an HbA1c of 6.5%
or higher. Prediabetes is generally indicated by an FBG of 100-125 mg/dL, an OGTT
2-hour value of 140-199 mg/dL, or an HbA1c of 5.7-6.4%.

Glycemic Targets
For most non-pregnant adults with diabetes, a reasonable HbA1c target is below
7.0%, though targets are individualized based on age, comorbidities, risk of
hypoglycemia, and disease duration. Less stringent targets (for example below
8.0%) may be appropriate for patients with limited life expectancy or extensive
comorbid conditions.

Lifestyle Management
Lifestyle intervention is a cornerstone of diabetes management. This typically
includes structured nutrition guidance, regular physical activity (commonly at
least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity activity), weight management
where appropriate, and smoking cessation support. Diabetes self-management
education and support programs are associated with improved glycemic control.

Pharmacologic Management
Metformin is commonly recommended as an initial pharmacologic agent for type 2
diabetes in the absence of contraindications, alongside lifestyle modification.
Additional agents (such as SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4
inhibitors, sulfonylureas, or insulin) may be added based on individual factors
including cardiovascular and renal risk, hypoglycemia risk, weight considerations,
and cost.

Monitoring
Regular HbA1c testing (typically every 3-6 months depending on control and
treatment changes) is recommended to assess long-term glycemic control.
Self-monitoring of blood glucose may be recommended for patients on insulin
therapy or those at risk of hypoglycemia.

Complications and Screening
Chronic hyperglycemia is associated with microvascular complications (retinopathy,
nephropathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular complications (cardiovascular disease,
stroke, peripheral artery disease). Regular screening is recommended, including
annual dilated eye exams, annual urine albumin and serum creatinine testing to
assess kidney function, and periodic foot examinations. Lipid profile monitoring
and cardiovascular risk assessment are also part of routine diabetes care.

Kidney Function Markers
Serum creatinine and urine albumin (or albumin-to-creatinine ratio) are commonly
used to screen for diabetic kidney disease. Elevated urine albumin
(microalbuminuria: roughly 30-300 mg/g; macroalbuminuria: above 300 mg/g) can be
an early indicator of kidney involvement and warrants closer monitoring and, where
appropriate, treatment adjustment (e.g., ACE inhibitors or ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors).
